# Triage System

## Why Seven Categories

Five is too few — you end up merging things that feel very different in practice, like workout music and party music, or sad music and chill evening music. Ten is too many — you start hesitating over where songs belong, and that hesitation is the death of any system you actually want to maintain. Seven hits the point where every category is instantly recognisable, distinct from every other, and between them they cover every kind of music you will ever encounter.

---

## The Framework

Categories are not based on genre. They are based on the intersection of three things:

1. **Energy level** — how activating or calming the music is
2. **Emotional valence** — how dark or bright the music feels
3. **Situational function** — what role the music plays in your life when you choose to listen to it

Genre is completely irrelevant. A jazz track and an ambient electronic track might sit in the same category. Two hip hop tracks might sit in completely different categories. The question is never *what kind of music is this* — it is always *when would I reach for this, and what does it do for me when I play it.*

### The Two Axes

The two foundational axes come from music psychology research, which consistently finds that how humans experience music maps onto two dimensions more cleanly than onto any genre taxonomy:

- **Vertical axis — Energy (arousal):** runs from still and calm at the bottom to intense and activating at the top
- **Horizontal axis — Valence:** runs from dark, heavy, and emotionally weighty on the left to bright, positive, and uplifting on the right

Every emotional state you can name is a position on this grid. Melancholy is low energy, low valence. Euphoria is high energy, high valence. Aggression is high energy, low valence. Serenity is low energy, high valence.

The seven categories are clusters on this grid, shaped by the third factor — situational function — which is what stops this from being a purely abstract mood map and makes it practical.

## The Seven Categories

### 1. Lock In
**Energy:** Medium–low | **Valence:** Neutral

Focus music. The defining characteristic is that it occupies just enough of your attention to block out distraction without ever competing with your actual thinking. You put this on when you are coding, writing, studying, doing deep work of any kind. The music should not have a strong emotional opinion — it should not make you feel sad or happy or pumped up. It should create a texture of concentration.

Typical sounds: lo-fi hip hop beats, ambient music, post-rock instrumentals, classical piano or strings, minimal techno, instrumental jazz. Vocal music can work here if the vocals are treated as texture rather than language (e.g. shoegaze, certain dream pop), but as a general rule: if you find yourself listening to the words, it belongs somewhere else.

---

### 2. Drive
**Energy:** High | **Valence:** Neutral–positive (intense but forward-moving)

High-energy momentum music. This is what you put on for workouts, running, driving fast, getting hyped before a big event — any situation where you need physical or psychological momentum. The defining quality is tempo and forward motion. The music should feel like it is pushing you.

Genre is completely irrelevant. Aggressive rap, drum and bass, high-tempo EDM, punk rock, power pop, and intense pop all sit together because they all serve the same function.

**Distinction from Glow:** Both are energetic, but Drive is about pushing *harder* while Glow is about floating *higher*. You would never put Drive on at a dinner party and you would never run a marathon to Glow.

---

### 3. Glow
**Energy:** Medium–high | **Valence:** Strongly positive

Bright, sociable, feel-good music. The "everything's alright" register. You put it on when you are cooking with friends, having pre-drinks, walking on a sunny morning, hosting a casual gathering — anything where the vibe should be warm, open, and social. The music should make the room feel lighter.

Typical sounds: pop, funk, disco, upbeat indie, summery R&B, afrobeats, reggae, soul.

Sorting criterion: could you put this on at a relaxed social gathering and it would make people feel good without demanding attention? If it energises the room without dominating it, it is Glow.

---

### 4. Unwind
**Energy:** Low | **Valence:** Positive–neutral

Low-energy music for winding down. This is what you put on in the evening when the day is done — reading, cooking alone, having a quiet night in, decompressing. The music should feel like a warm blanket. It is not trying to make you feel anything intense; it is giving you permission to stop.

Typical sounds: soft indie, acoustic singer-songwriter, neo-soul, chill jazz, bossa nova, easy listening electronic, mellow R&B.

**Distinction from Lock In:** Lock In is functional and productivity-oriented. Unwind is restorative and leisure-oriented. You would not work to Unwind music — it is too warm and inviting and would make you want to stop. You would not relax to Lock In music — it is too neutral and texturally flat to feel like a reward.

**Distinction from Feels:** Both can be quiet and gentle, but Unwind is emotionally neutral by design — it is comfort. Feels is emotionally loaded — it wants you to engage. Bon Iver quietly breaking your heart is Feels. A soft jazz piano trio in the background is Unwind.

---

### 5. Feels
**Energy:** Any | **Valence:** Dark or emotionally complex

Emotionally heavy music. This is the category for music you put on when you actually want to *feel* something — to process, reflect, wallow, be moved, sit with sadness or nostalgia or bittersweetness. The defining characteristic is emotional weight and intentionality. You do not put Feels on in the background. You put it on because you want to engage with the emotion it carries.

Typical sounds: ballads, confessional singer-songwriter, sad indie, blues, emotionally heavy hip hop, orchestral pieces with strong emotional arcs, post-punk with melancholy undertones.

Sorting criterion: does the music make you feel something in your chest? If it is reaching for an emotional response rather than serving a functional purpose, it is Feels. Covers everything from quiet devastating folk songs to loud cathartic rock.

---

### 6. Edge
**Energy:** High | **Valence:** Dark/negative (top-left of the grid)

High-energy, dark, intense, confrontational music. This is not sad music — it is *heavy* music. Aggressive, forceful, sometimes angry, sometimes just relentlessly dark and powerful. You put it on when you want to feel dangerous, when you are in a dark headspace and want music that matches it rather than lifts you out.

Typical sounds: metal, hardcore and underground rap, industrial, drill, darkwave, aggressive electronic, noise rock, heavy post-punk.

**Distinction from Drive:** The emotional colour of the intensity is different. Drive says *let's go*. Edge says *watch out*. If the intensity is dark, weighted, confrontational, or menacing — it is Edge. If the intensity makes you feel powerful in a straightforwardly positive way — it is Drive.

---

### 7. Night
**Energy:** Medium | **Valence:** Slightly dark/atmospheric

Late, atmospheric music for after midnight. Sits in the middle of the energy axis and slightly left of centre on valence — a space none of the other categories cover. This is what you put on for late night drives, intimate moments, 2am headphone sessions, any context where the world has gotten smaller and quieter and you want music that fits that contracted space.

Typical sounds: deep house, downtempo electronic, R&B slow jams, trip hop, dream pop, dark ambient with rhythm, certain kinds of jazz.

Sorting criterion: if the music feels wrong at noon on a Tuesday but perfect at 1am on a Saturday — it is Night.

**Distinction from Unwind:** Unwind is about *resting*. Night is about *inhabiting the dark hours*. Unwind music could play at 7pm after work. Night music belongs after the world has gone quiet.

**Distinction from Feels:** Feels wants you to engage emotionally. Night wants you to sink into an atmosphere. Night is not about processing feelings — it is about existing in a vibe.

---

## Decision Process for Triage

When categorising any track, ask three questions in order:

1. **What is the energy level?** — calm, medium, or intense
2. **What is the emotional colour?** — dark, neutral, or bright
3. **What situation does this serve?** — when would I reach for this?

The answers to all three together should point clearly to one category. If a track could plausibly sit in two categories, the tiebreaker is always the third question — **situational function**.

Example: a track that is medium energy and slightly dark could be Night or Feels. If you would put it on to sink into a late-night atmosphere — Night. If you would put it on to sit with an emotion — Feels. The function resolves the ambiguity.

---

## Genre Is Not a Signal

The system is designed so that every genre has a home but no genre maps exclusively to one category:

- **Jazz** → spans Lock In, Unwind, Night, and Feels depending on the track
- **Hip hop** → spans Drive, Glow, Feels, and Edge
- **Electronic** → spans every single category
- **Pop** → spans Drive, Glow, and Feels

Genre tells you what instruments are playing. These categories tell you what the music is *doing for you*.

## Claude Prompt Strategy

The system prompt given to Claude for triage should:
1. Explain the three-axis framework (energy, valence, function)
2. Define all seven categories using the descriptions above — full detail, not a summary
3. Emphasise that genre is not a signal; function and feel are
4. Explain the key distinctions (Drive vs Glow, Unwind vs Feels, Night vs Feels, Drive vs Edge)
5. Instruct Claude to return exactly one category name per track from the valid set

Input per track: `title | artist | album`
Output per track: exactly one of `Lock In`, `Drive`, `Glow`, `Unwind`, `Feels`, `Edge`, `Night`
